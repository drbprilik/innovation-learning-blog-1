Why Resilience Is No Longer Optional — For Any Child
The Why, What & How of Resilience-Centered Education

Every Child Needs This.
Not Just the Struggling Ones.

Resilience research was born in crisis — studying children navigating poverty, trauma, instability. But the world has changed. The conditions that once defined "at-risk" now touch every child in some form. What the research built for the margins turns out to be essential for everyone.

For School Administrators & Families · Child Development & Academic Readiness · 2025

For a long time, resilience was a word we associated with kids in hard circumstances. Children navigating instability at home. Children in under-resourced communities. The research that built our understanding of resilience — Emmy Werner's landmark Kauai study, Ann Masten's decades of work, the Circle of Courage developed by Brendtro, Brokenleg, and Van Bockern — came largely from studying children who had every reason not to thrive and somehow did anyway. That research was important. It still is. But if we keep framing resilience as something only certain kids need, we're missing what is right in front of us: the world has changed in ways that make resilience a requirement for every child growing up in it.

This is not a sky-is-falling argument. Children are not fragile. Most are doing fine. But "fine" and "fully equipped for what's coming" are different things — and the gap between them is wider than it used to be, for more kids than we tend to admit.

WHY The World Every Child Is Actually Growing Up In

Start with the pandemic. Schools closed for months — in some places, well over a year. Children lost not just instruction time but something harder to recover: the daily practice of being with other people. Of navigating conflict, building friendships, reading a room. Social skills and emotional regulation aren't just personality traits — they're practiced capacities, and millions of children had that practice interrupted at a critical developmental window. Research now puts average pandemic learning loss at somewhere between half a year and a full year of academic progress. The emotional and social losses are harder to quantify, but anyone who works with kids knows they're real and they're still playing out.

Then there's social media. It arrived in children's lives faster than any adult had time to think carefully about what it would do. What it does — and the research on this is increasingly consistent — is subject children to an unrelenting stream of social comparison at exactly the age when identity is most fragile. Forty percent of high school students reported persistent feelings of sadness or hopelessness in the CDC's most recent Youth Risk Behavior Survey. That is not a fringe statistic. It's four out of ten teenagers, across every demographic, every zip code. And it's been climbing steadily for over a decade.

And beyond those specific pressures, there's the general pace of things. The world these children are inheriting moves faster and shifts more often than the one that produced the education system we're still largely running. Jobs that didn't exist five years ago are now in demand. Jobs that seem secure today may not exist in ten years. AI is reshaping what work looks like — and what skills actually matter — in real time. No curriculum can track that. What can keep up is a child who has been taught to keep going when things change, to tolerate uncertainty, to recover from failure and try again. That's resilience. And it's not a remedial skill. It's the skill of the century.

This Isn't Just About Struggling Kids

Here is what gets overlooked: the student who is doing fine — good grades, stable home, supportive parents — is often succeeding on borrowed infrastructure. The structure around them has caught them every time before they had to catch themselves. Move them to a harder environment — a more demanding school, a college campus where no one is tracking whether they show up, a workplace that rewards genuine self-direction — and some of them discover they are less equipped than their record suggested.

Resilience isn't remediation. It's what makes a successful student genuinely stronger at the next level. More creative. Willing to take intellectual risks. Able to fail at something and not fall apart. Every child deserves that. Not just the ones we're worried about.

WHAT What Resilience Actually Is — and What Every Child Needs to Build It

Resilience isn't toughness. It's not the ability to endure difficulty without showing it. Ann Masten, one of the most cited researchers in the field, called it "ordinary magic" — the predictable output of ordinary conditions that any child can access when the adults around them build those conditions deliberately. The research is clear on what those conditions are. They've been documented across cultures, across decades, across every kind of adversity researchers have studied.

The Circle of Courage — a framework developed by Dr. Larry Brendtro, Dr. Martin Brokenleg, and Dr. Steve Van Bockern, drawing on developmental psychology and Indigenous traditions of child-rearing — distills that research into four universal needs. Every child. Not just the ones struggling. Every one.

Belonging

The need to be known — genuinely, not administratively. A child who doesn't feel they belong in a learning environment is spending energy on self-protection that should go toward learning. Belonging isn't a feeling. It's a condition that has to be actively maintained.

Mastery

The experience of real struggle followed by real success. A child who has never had to work through genuine difficulty has no evidence that effort changes outcomes. When difficulty finally arrives — and it always does — they have nothing to draw on.

Independence

The capacity to direct oneself. Children who are always told what to do, think, and feel never build the internal compass that adult life requires. Independence is practiced, not granted — and programs that don't create space for it are leaving a gap.

Generosity

Connection to something larger than oneself. Viktor Frankl identified this as one of the most powerful human resilience factors: the belief that your existence matters beyond your own immediate needs. Children who experience this regularly are fundamentally more grounded.

When these four conditions are consistently present, something measurable happens. Hundreds of studies involving more than a million students have now connected resilience and social-emotional development to academic outcomes. The numbers are not subtle. A landmark meta-analysis of 213 programs found an average 11-percentile-point gain in academic achievement — just from building these conditions deliberately. A 2025 study from Yale and USC, covering 33,000 students across 40 programs, found gains that widened to 8.4 percentile points in programs lasting longer than a semester. Attendance improves. Engagement improves. And the students who were already doing well get better — not marginally, but genuinely, in the ways that matter at the next level.

"Resilience does not emerge from exceptional children. It emerges from ordinary environments that consistently provide the right conditions." Ann Masten · University of Minnesota · Resilience Researcher
HOW What This Actually Looks Like in a School

Knowing what children need is the easy part. The harder question is what to actually do about it — in a real classroom, a real hallway, with real kids who are tired and distracted and sometimes difficult. The REACH model was built to answer that question. Not as a philosophy. As an operational framework: specific, trainable practices that any adult can learn and any school or program can implement with intention. The setting changes. What children need doesn't.

REACH Five Conditions. Every Child. Every Day.
R

Relationships First

Werner's sixty-year study found that the single most reliable predictor of resilience was one caring adult who genuinely believed in the child. Not a program — a person. Which means the most important hiring decision a school leader makes is not about credentials. It's about disposition. The adult who moves toward kids, not away. Who remembers what a child told them last Tuesday. Who is glad to be there on a hard day as much as an easy one.

One interview question reveals this more reliably than any resume: "Tell me about a child you found genuinely difficult to connect with — and what you did about it." The answer tells you whether that person understands that the hardest child to reach is the one who needs reaching most. In practice, Relationships First means every child is greeted by name at the door — every day, by an adult who is present. Phones away. Eyes up. Not a policy. A posture that has to be hired for, trained, and held as a non-negotiable standard.

E

Environment by Design

Children read spaces before they read people. A room that is organized and clearly waiting for students delivers a message before anyone speaks: you were expected here, and someone cared enough to get ready for you. That lowers cortisol. It signals safety. A nervous system that feels safe learns — one on alert protects itself instead.

In practice: materials are set before students arrive. The day's schedule is posted in specific, engaging language — not "work time" but "engineering challenge: redesign the school lunch line." Zones are defined. The room itself is doing part of the work before the bell rings. Schools that take this seriously find that behavioral incidents at transitions decrease — not because rules changed, but because the environment stopped creating the chaos that made transitions hard in the first place.

A

Activate Belonging

Belonging doesn't happen on its own in a room full of kids who didn't choose to be together. It has to be built — every day, for every child, including the one at the edge of the group who has learned that belonging isn't safe and pushes it away when it gets close. That child is the real test of whether a school is doing this work.

Activating belonging looks like: morning circles where every student is seen before instruction starts. Cooperative structures where no one succeeds without the group. Adults who scan the room and notice — really notice — who is on the margins, and move toward them with genuine curiosity, not a clipboard. Schools can build this into their culture with a simple professional standard: no child ends the day unseen by at least one adult who actually knows them.

C

Consistency Creates Safety

Safety accumulates through reliable experiences. The same warm greeting on a hard Thursday as on a good Monday. The same structure, the same response, the same adult showing up the same way regardless of what is happening elsewhere in the building. For children navigating unpredictability at home — or in their heads courtesy of social media — a consistent school environment is not a management preference. It is a clinical intervention.

Practically: predictable daily rhythms students can count on. Adults who maintain the same tone under pressure. When something changes — a schedule shift, a substitute, a disruption — someone explains it, because children shouldn't be expected to absorb chaos silently. Schools that build consistency intentionally discover something counterintuitive: more structure creates more genuine freedom. Children stop spending energy bracing for what's coming next and put it toward learning instead.

H

Have Purpose in Everything

When students understand why they're doing something — not just what — they stop complying and start investing. This is the difference between a student who does a service project because it's required and one who understands that contributing beyond themselves is the point — and carries that into the next hard thing they face.

In schools, this means every adult can articulate the developmental reason behind every activity, not just the academic one. Homework support is structured so students try before they ask — building the experience of productive struggle, which is the raw material of mastery. Physical activity is chosen for cooperation, not elimination. And when a student asks "why are we doing this?" the answer goes beyond the lesson plan. When every adult in a building can answer that question with something true and specific, the culture of the school shifts in ways that standardized tests eventually catch up to.

Designing the Environment — and the People In It

What REACH makes clear is that resilience isn't a program you bolt onto an existing school. It's an environment you design — and environments are made of people as much as spaces and schedules. A school can post the right values on the wall and hire adults who quietly undermine them every day. The design has to go all the way down: to how you write a job description, what you ask in an interview, what you celebrate publicly, and what you hold as a non-negotiable standard even when it's inconvenient.

Imagine a school where every adult greets every child by name at the start of every day. Where classrooms are ready before students arrive. Where a struggling student has at least two adults who notice when something is off — and know enough about that child to say something true. Where cooperative challenge is a regular feature of the week, and service to others is built into the rhythm of the year. That school exists — in pieces, in many good schools already. REACH is the framework that makes it intentional across the whole building, for every child, every day.

A Framework Built From Research — Designed for Practice

The REACH model was developed by Dr. Brett Prilik, drawing on sixty years of resilience research, the Circle of Courage framework, and direct experience designing and running programs for children across a range of school communities. It was built for the real world — not ideal conditions, but actual schools and actual adults who need something they can learn, practice, and hold each other accountable to.

What the research established studying children in adversity, REACH makes available to every child in every environment. Because the conditions it builds — belonging, mastery, independence, generosity, consistency, relationship — are not remedial. They are universal. They are what every child needs to become someone who can handle a harder world than the one they were born into. And building them is not as complicated as it sounds. It requires intention. It requires training. It requires adults who were hired partly for their capacity to do this work. And it requires leadership willing to hold it as a standard rather than an aspiration.

The question is not whether this is possible. The question is whether we are ready to expect it.

About the Author

Dr. Brett Prilik holds an Ed.D. from the University of Northern Colorado with emphasis in school administration and special education. A former teacher, building administrator, and district-level administrator, he has served as adjunct faculty at the University of Northern Colorado's School of Education and has been involved in the development of six school programs across public school and nonprofit settings. He is the founder and CEO of Innovation Learning and the developer of the REACH framework.

His published works include his doctoral dissertation, Resiliency in Public School Teachers of Students with Emotional Disabilities (University of Northern Colorado), as well as 125 Days: Every Child Already Has a Second School Year and Resilience Is Engineered, Not Hoped For — both available at Innovation Learning.
